Introduction

Mary Grande left a three-room house near Novo Mesto in 1920, aged ten. Her mother ran it alone — farming, keeping cattle, trading them — while her father had been in the United States since 1911. S1.014

The journey runs on people rather than institutions. When the family's clothes are stolen in Trieste, it is “a fellow that my mother knew” who buys replacements in London. S1.017

What she returns to most is not hardship but texture: the barn under the same roof, a pet cow she “loved very much.” S2.031 That warmth sits oddly beside this project's cost-frame, and is not resolved here.

Version 2 · after Rodwin

Across both interviews, migration is financed less with money than with people. Households split themselves across the Atlantic, and the women who stay run what remains S1.014. The journey's emergencies are met through whoever the family can reach S1.017. What the account cannot yet hold is the warmth: both narrators return to animals, rooms and routines with an affection the cost-frame has no place for S2.031.

Grande Well, my father was here in the States since 1911. In fact, he was, during World War One, here in the United States, and my mother was a farmer, and she raised some cattle and bartered with them.

Grande But part of our clothes was stolen in Trieste, so a fellow that my mother knew, he went in, I believe, in London, to buy some clothes for us. He could speak American.

Grande It was an average peasant home. It was three rooms, living room and kitchen in the middle and a small room on the side. And then facing what would be, I guess, the east side was half, was a barn where the cattle were.

Grande Oh, yes. I enjoyed it because they were part of my life. In fact, I had a pet cow that I loved very much. It was just a regular home life between the animals.
