DP-40 · Mary Yankovik Grande Yugoslavia, 1920 · age 10 · interviewed 1989 page 1 of 6

Phillips: Before you tell us about travelling from your home country to the United States, could you tell us a little bit about what your home was like.

Grande: Well, my father was here in the States since 1911. In fact, he was, during World War One, here in the United States, and my mother was a farmer, and she raised some cattle and bartered with them.

two readings
split-household migration economy
standard
Household sustained across continents: one earner abroad, farm and barter at home.
women's labour sustaining male migration
critical
The mother's farming and barter underwrite the father's absence — work the narrative treats as background.

Grande: It was an average peasant home. It was three rooms, living room and kitchen in the middle and a small room on the side. And then facing what would be, I guess, the east side was half, was a barn where the cattle were.

Grande: Oh, yes. I enjoyed it because they were part of my life. In fact, I had a pet cow that I loved very much. And then the pigs were, the pig sty was just across from the kitchen door, just a little ways away. So all the animals were right close to the house. It was just a regular home life between the animals.

animals as companions in remembered childhood
phenomenological
Cohabitation with animals recalled as affection and ordinariness, not hardship.

Grande: But part of our clothes was stolen in Trieste, so a fellow that my mother knew, he went in, I believe, in London, to buy some clothes for us. He could speak American. He came back from the States and who he bought back I don't remember. But, uh, he bought some clothes so we had something to change in to, to wear.

reliance on kin and acquaintance networks in transit
